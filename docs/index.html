What a Class Description Buys

A zero-shot classifier is handed the names of its classes and nothing else. Rewriting those names into real definitions is usually treated as prompt-fiddling. It is not: what a description buys is alignment with the vocabulary of the documents it must attract — and how much that is worth can be predicted before the classifier is run. On German trade-register texts it is worth +26.8 points. On newsgroup posts, where the class name is already the documents' own word, it is worth nothing.

A register of claims

This project classifies entries in a German Handelsregister. What follows is its own register: every substantive claim the work made, in the order it was made, with what became of it. Two entries record hypotheses of mine that the evidence destroyed. One records a headline number I withdrew. They are here because a result you cannot see me trying to break is not a result.

01
Retracted

“The system reaches 80.0% Top-1 accuracy” — as first reported.

That figure came from 30 documents I had written myself to exercise the pipeline. It measured how well the system classified my own idea of a company description, not the register. Re-measured on 299 documents sampled from the corpus and labelled under a written protocol, the same system scores 52.5%.

The companion Top-3 figure of 83.3% was worse than optimistic — it was an artefact. The classifier filtered candidates at a confidence threshold before the top-3 cut, so for 25 of those 30 documents fewer than three candidates survived and “Top-3” was scoring a list shorter than three. Removing the filter, the same run gives 96.7%. Both numbers were meaningless; the evaluation set was the real defect.

02
Falsified

“The descriptions underperform because they are in the wrong language.”

The taxonomy's original class descriptions were terse and mostly Turkish; the documents are German. Rewriting them in German improved matters a great deal, and the obvious reading was that the encoder had been paying a translation penalty.

The obvious reading was wrong. I wrote a control condition — the same terse content, rendered literally into German, with no new information added. It did not help. It was 2.3 points worse, and the difference did not clear significance. The entire gain had come from the content I added while translating, not from the language I added it in. A 2×2 of document-language against description-language confirmed it: language match is not what moves the score.

The three conditions below hold everything else fixed and drop the seed keyword lists from the class vector entirely, so that the description is the only thing varying.

26.1%same content, literal German
52.8%rewritten as definitions

03
Falsified

“A class gains in proportion to how absent its name is from its documents.”

This was my explanation for why some classes gained enormously from elaboration and others not at all, and at the corpus level it looks compelling: classes whose names never appear in their own documents gain 8.1 points on average against 1.4 for the rest.

Tested properly — per class, across all 32 classes of two corpora — it collapses. The correlation is negative, small, and insignificant. The corpus-level split was real but it was not the mechanism; lexical overlap sorts the two regimes without explaining anything inside either one. The hypothesis had to be replaced, which is what entry 04 does.

ρ = −0.16name overlap vs. gain (p = 0.37)

04
Supported

“A class gains in proportion to how far its description moves toward its documents.”

The replacement is measurable without any labels at prediction time. For a class c, alignment is the cosine between its description vector and the centroid of the documents belonging to it. Elaborating the description changes that alignment by some amount, and that change is what predicts the gain.

Across 32 classes the correlation is positive and significant, and — this is the part that matters — it holds within each corpus separately, so it is not an artefact of one corpus being easier than the other. A description that does not move its class toward its documents buys nothing, however well written.

ρ = +0.513alignment change vs. gain (p = 0.003)
+0.556 / +0.634within NACE / within 20NG
n = 32classes
05
Predicted, then confirmed

“Then it must work on a corpus I have not looked at yet.”

A rule fitted to two corpora explains those two corpora. To be worth anything it has to say something risky about a third. Reuters-21578 is the right test because its labels are opaque codes — money-fx, acq, dlr — which puts it in the NACE regime rather than the readable-name regime of 20 Newsgroups.

So I measured the alignment change first, wrote down what it implied, and only then looked at accuracy. The measured change was +0.028: positive, so elaboration should help, but small, so it should help far less than on NACE. It helped by 4.8 points — positive, significant, and an order of magnitude below NACE's 26.8, exactly as the size of the alignment change required. The script prints the prediction before it computes the accuracy, so the order cannot be quietly rearranged after the fact.

+0.028alignment change, measured first
+4.8 ppaccuracy gain (p = 0.0005)

06
Falsified

“The pipeline the project was named after contributes something.”

The project began as a guided keyword extractor: seed keyword lists per sector, a seed-guided extraction step, and a six-stage information-theoretic keyword filter. Ablating each of them, on 299 documents, changes the score by nothing that can be distinguished from noise — all three at p = 1.000.

Ablating the class descriptions instead costs 8.4 points, and reverting to the project's first taxonomy — the terse, mostly Turkish one — costs 17.7. So the component nobody thought of as the method is the method, and three components that took most of the engineering are inert. The repository still carries its original directory name; the framing has moved to what the evidence supports.

The rule, across three corpora

Each corpus sits where its alignment change puts it. The relationship is monotone, and Reuters was placed on this axis before its accuracy was known. Three points cannot carry a correlation on their own — the statistical claim is the per-class one in entry 04, ρ = +0.513 over 32 classes. Where it fails

142 of 299 documents are misclassified. 87 of those still hold the right section inside the top three, so most failures are ranking failures rather than blindness.

I hand-coded 50 errors from the development half against a written codebook, leaving the held-out half untouched: 17 seed leakage, 14 genuinely ambiguous section boundaries, 10 companies that really do operate in several sections, 8 taxonomy granularity, 1 pure boilerplate.

How the labels were made

299 documents sampled stratified from 9,993 register records. Labels are model-assisted and human-validated under a two-stage protocol: a blind pilot, adjudication, then a fresh measurement sample.

Agreement between the human pass and the silver labels is κ = 0.772. That figure is quoted next to every result computed on those labels, because it is the ceiling on what any of them mean.

What it is built on

paraphrase-multilingual-MiniLM-L12-v2, 384 dimensions, no fine-tuning and no labelled training data. Seeded throughout.

The corpus cannot be redistributed, so the repository commits the vocabulary and IDF statistics needed to reproduce the TF-IDF baseline exactly without it. 141 tests, reproducible from a clean checkout.

The finding I am most confident in is the one I tried hardest to destroy, and the two hypotheses I lost along the way are in the record above rather than deleted from it.
